Frequently asked questions

Common questions about breathing exercises

There is no single correct way to breathe. Different rhythms suit different situations and different people. If a technique causes discomfort, you do not need to endure it for the sake of getting the numbers right — speed up, remove the pause, or choose another rhythm.

Do I need to take a deep breath?

Usually not.

“Deep breathing” is often used as another name for calm breathing, but they are not quite the same. Taking one very large breath after another, especially during anxiety or panic, can worsen hyperventilation and the unpleasant sensations that come with it.

Think less about breathing as deeply as possible and more about breathing calmly and gently. The inhale should not require effort.

Should I breathe into my belly?

You do not need to force your abdomen to move.

During calm breathing, the diaphragm works naturally, so the abdomen and lower rib cage usually expand a little on the inhale. There is no need to push the belly out strongly or monitor every movement.

What matters is that breathing remains easy and does not become one more task you have to perform perfectly.

Is it better to breathe through the nose or mouth?

If it is comfortable, usually through the nose.

The nose warms, humidifies, and filters the air, and calm nasal breathing often makes it easier to avoid breaths that are too large.

But nasal breathing is not a requirement. If your nose is blocked or breathing through the mouth feels easier right now, do not fight yourself for the sake of the exercise.

Does the exhale have to be longer than the inhale?

No.

Many people find a longer exhale pleasant, and it is common in relaxation exercises. But it is not a universal rule.

Slow, even breathing can also help lower arousal. If equal inhales and exhales feel more comfortable, that is a perfectly valid option.

How slowly should I breathe?

As slowly as remains comfortable.

There is no benefit in jumping straight from ordinary breathing to very long cycles. If you have to wait for the next inhale, strongly want to breathe sooner, or feel that you are not getting enough air, the chosen rhythm is probably too slow for you right now.

Start faster and slow down gradually if you want to.

What if I feel short of breath?

Do not try to stretch the breath even further. Switch to a shorter cycle and breathe more naturally. Sometimes the feeling of air hunger appears precisely because someone is trying too hard to control their breathing.

If marked shortness of breath begins unexpectedly, does not ease after you stop the exercise, or comes with chest pain, fainting, blue lips, or other unusual symptoms, this is no longer a matter of adjusting the practice — seek medical care.

Do I need to hold my breath?

Not necessarily.

A hold is part of some breathing patterns, not an essential part of breathing practice.

Some people enjoy pauses, while others find them uncomfortable or feel an urge to inhale. If holding your breath feels unpleasant, choose exercises without pauses.

What if the exercise makes me dizzy?

Stop deliberately slowing or deepening your breath and breathe normally for a while. Dizziness can occur if you start breathing too deeply and are effectively hyperventilating. The answer is not one more “correct deep breath,” but a return to calm, natural breathing.

If the dizziness is severe, unusual, does not pass, or happens regularly outside breathing exercises, discuss it with a doctor.

How long should I continue?

There is no single correct duration. Sometimes one or two minutes is enough to feel a little steadier. At other times, five or ten minutes feels comfortable. You do not have to finish just because you started a timer. If the effect you needed has already arrived, you can stop.

What if I keep losing the rhythm?

That is fine. Begin again with the next inhale. A breathing exercise is not a test of your sense of rhythm. You can get distracted, inhale early, skip a pause, or forget which cycle you are on.

That is why Breath Slow shows the rhythm on screen: you do not need to remember a sequence or count seconds. Simply return to the movement and continue.

What if breathing exercises do not help me at all?

That happens too.

Breathing is one tool, not a universal answer to every state. It does not have to work for every person in every situation.

If one rhythm feels unpleasant, try another. If focusing on breathing is itself irritating, movement, a walk, grounding, music, or another way of shifting attention may suit you better.

You do not need to force yourself to breathe “correctly” just because breathing exercises are supposed to calm everyone.
